Respecting Others' Work and Avoiding Plagiarism

Copying someone else's work and pretending it's yours is called plagiarism — and it's one of the most serious mistakes you can make in school. In this lesson, you'll learn how to quote, paraphrase, and cite sources properly so you can use other people's ideas honestly and respectfully.

Whose Words Are These?

Imagine you're reading a school essay and you see this paragraph:

📄
"Regular exercise improves heart health, reduces stress, and helps people live longer, healthier lives. It is one of the most important habits for overall wellbeing."

The paragraph sounds great — but here's the question: Did the student write this themselves, or did they copy it from a website?

If they copied it word-for-word without giving credit, that's plagiarism — and it's a serious problem. Even if the words are "true," you still can't use someone else's words as your own.

💡
Key idea: When you use someone else's words or ideas, you must give credit. This is called academic honesty.
What is Plagiarism?

Plagiarism is using someone else's work — their words, ideas, images, or data — and presenting it as your own, without giving proper credit.

Plagiarism includes:

  • Copying text from a website, book, or article without citing it
  • Using someone's idea without mentioning them as the source
  • Copying and pasting from any source into your work
  • Reusing someone else's homework, essay, or project
  • Buying or downloading work online and claiming it as your own
  • Paraphrasing too closely — changing only one or two words
💡
Remember: Even if it's just one sentence copied from a source — if you don't give credit, it's plagiarism. There are no "small" cases.
Why Is Plagiarism Wrong?

Plagiarism hurts in more ways than one. Here's why it matters:

Who Is Affected How They Are Affected
The Original Creator Their work is being stolen. They don't get credit or respect.
You (the Student) You miss the chance to actually learn. You risk failing grades or school discipline.
Your Teacher They can't tell what you've actually learned or what you need help with.
Your School Loses its reputation for academic honesty and fairness.
Your Future A plagiarism record can affect college admissions and jobs.
💡
Most importantly: Plagiarism robs you of the chance to actually learn. The goal of school isn't just to finish the assignment — it's to become smarter.

Three Ways to Use Sources Honestly

When you use someone else's work in your project, you have three honest options. Each one has a purpose and requires citation:

💬

1. Quote

Use the exact words, inside quotation marks, with the author's name.
Best for: definitions, powerful statements, exact data.

🖊️

2. Paraphrase

Restate the idea in your own words, but still cite the source.
Best for: explaining concepts in your own voice.

💭

3. Summarize

Give a short overview of the main ideas, with citation.
Best for: covering a lot of information in a few sentences.

📚

4. Cite

Always give credit — even for paraphrased or summarized content. Include a reference list at the end.

Quote vs Paraphrase vs Plagiarize

Let's compare three ways a student might use the same source. The original source says:

📖
Original: "Regular exercise improves heart health." — Smith, J. (2023). Health Basics. Wellness Press.
Version Correct or Wrong?
Version A (Plagiarism):
"Regular exercise improves heart health."
(no citation) 		❌ PLAGIARISM. Exact words with no citation.
Version B (Quote):
"Regular exercise improves heart health" (Smith, 2023). 		✅ CORRECT. Exact words in quotation marks with citation.
Version C (Paraphrase):
Working out regularly strengthens your heart (Smith, 2023). 		✅ CORRECT. Rewritten in your own words with citation.
Version D (Too close):
Regular exercise makes heart health better (Smith, 2023). 		⚠️ RISKY. Too close to original. Rewrite more completely.
💡
Paraphrasing tip: Read the original, look away, and write the idea in your own words from memory. Then check the original to make sure you didn't copy the structure.
How to Cite Sources

Citations have two parts: an in-text citation where you use the source, and a reference list at the end of your work.

Part 1 — In-text citation (simple format):

In-Text Citation
(Author, Year)

Part 2 — Reference list entry (simple format):

Reference Format
Author. (Year). Title. Source. URL

Example citation:

📝
In-text: According to Smith (2023), regular exercise is important for heart health.

Reference list:
Smith, J. (2023). Health Basics. Wellness Press. https://www.example.com/health-basics

Consequences of Plagiarism

Plagiarism has real consequences. Depending on how serious the case is, this could happen:

  1. Lower grade or zero on the assignment.
    The teacher may not give any credit for copied work.
  2. Fail the entire course.
    Repeated or serious plagiarism can lead to failing the class.
  3. School discipline.
    Detention, suspension, or expulsion in the most serious cases.
  4. Loss of trust.
    Teachers, parents, and classmates may not trust your work again.
  5. Long-term consequences.
    A plagiarism record can affect college admissions and future jobs.
💡
Don't risk it. Plagiarism takes less time to detect than to fix. Doing your own work is always the smarter, safer choice.

Activity — Cite It Right

Work with a partner. Choose a topic you're interested in (e.g., healthy eating, technology, environment, sports). Then:

  1. Find 3 sources online about your topic (articles, websites, videos, etc.).
  2. Write one direct quote from each source, with proper citation.
  3. Write one paraphrase of each source in your own words, with citation.
  4. Create a reference list at the end with all three sources.

Your reference format should be:

📚
Author. (Year). Title. Source. URL

Present your work to the class and explain how you avoided plagiarism.

Key Takeaway

Plagiarism is stealing someone's work — and it hurts everyone involved, especially you. But you don't have to avoid using sources — you just have to use them honestly. Quote exact words in quotation marks. Paraphrase ideas in your own words. And always cite your sources with an in-text citation and a reference list. Being honest in your work is a mark of integrity — and it makes your work stronger. Next lesson, you'll bring everything together in the final project!
