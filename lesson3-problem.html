Problem Statement and Purpose

Before you can solve a problem, you need to understand it clearly. A well-written problem statement tells you exactly what you're trying to do, what information you have, what result you want, and what limits you're working within. In this lesson, you'll learn how to write one like a pro.

Warm-Up: Vague vs Clear

Look at these two instructions:

❌

Instruction A

"Make food."

Too vague! What food? For whom? How much? Using what?

✅

Instruction B

"Make a sandwich using bread, tuna, and mayonnaise."

Clear! We know the ingredients and the goal.

Which one is clearer? Why?

  • Instruction A has no goal, no input, and no output.
  • Instruction B tells us the goal (make a sandwich), the input (bread, tuna, mayonnaise), and the output (a sandwich).
💡
Key idea: A clear problem statement helps us know which details are essential. Without one, we waste time on things that don't matter.
The Four Parts of a Problem Statement

Every clear problem statement has four parts. Think of them like the four corners of a strong table — if one is missing, the table wobbles.

🎯

1. Goal

What do we want to achieve? The purpose of the problem.

📥

2. Input

What information do we have? Numbers, measurements, data, or rules.

📤

3. Output

What do we want to produce? A plan, a number, a chart, an answer.

🚧

4. Constraints

What limits do we have? Time, money, size, safety, or rules.

Problem Statement Formula
We need to GOAL using INPUT to produce OUTPUT while CONSTRAINTS.

Worked Example: Arranging Chairs

Let's write a problem statement together. Here's the scenario:

Our school is holding an assembly. We have 200 students and 250 chairs. The hall is 20 m × 15 m. The fire exit must remain clear, and teachers need seats at the front.

Let's identify the four parts:

🎯

Goal

Arrange chairs for a school assembly.

📥

Input

200 students, 250 chairs, hall size 20 m × 15 m.

📤

Output

A seating plan.

🚧

Constraints

Fire exit must be clear. Teachers need seats at the front.

Now let's write the problem statement:

Problem Statement
We need to arrange chairs in a 20 m × 15 m hall using 200 students, 250 chairs, and hall dimensions to produce a seating plan while keeping the fire exit clear and reserving front seats for teachers.
💡
Notice: A clear problem statement helps us know exactly which details are essential. The color of the chairs, for example, isn't even mentioned — because it doesn't matter.

Why Purpose Matters

The purpose (or goal) is the most important part of a problem statement. If you don't know why you're doing something, you can't decide what details matter.

Purpose Essential Details Non-Essential Details
Arrange chairs for an assembly Number of students, hall size, fire exit Chair color, room temperature
Buy snacks for a class party Number of students, budget, allergies Snack brand, packaging color
Plan a school garden Plot size, sunlight, seed type Color of the watering can
Pack for a school trip Days, weather, activities Suitcase color, brand of shoes
💡
Remember: Different purposes need different details. That's why it's so important to identify the purpose first — before you decide anything else.

Activity — Write Your Own Problem Statement

Work with a partner. Choose one of the scenarios below (or create your own), then write a complete problem statement with all four parts.

  • Painting a classroom wall
  • Packing boxes for a school move
  • Planning a school garden
  • Buying supplies for a science experiment
  • Organizing a book fair

Your problem statement must include:

  1. Goal — What do you want to achieve?
  2. Input — What information do you have?
  3. Output — What do you want to produce?
  4. Constraints — What limits do you have?

Write your problem statement in the formula:

Your Template
We need to _____ using _____ to produce _____ while _____.

Then, list 5 essential details and 5 non-essential details for your problem. Explain why each is essential or not, using the three questions from Lesson 2.

Check Your Understanding

Answer these questions in your notebook:

  1. What are the four parts of a problem statement?
  2. What is the goal? Give an example.
  3. What is the difference between input and output?
  4. What is a constraint? Give three examples of constraints.
  5. Why does the purpose affect what details are essential?
  6. What happens if a problem statement is missing one of the four parts?

Key Takeaway

A clear problem statement has four parts: Goal, Input, Output, and Constraints. The purpose (goal) tells you what's important — and what you can ignore. Writing a clear problem statement is the first step to solving any problem well. Next lesson, you'll learn how to break problems down into step-by-step algorithms that anyone can follow.

Homework

Part 1 — Write a problem statement for a home task:

  • Choose a task at home (for example: cooking rice, cleaning your room, organizing your desk, or planning a family meal).
  • Write a complete problem statement with all four parts: Goal, Input, Output, Constraints.
  • Use the formula: "We need to ___ using ___ to produce ___ while ___."

Part 2 — List details:

  • Write 5 essential details for your task.
  • Write 5 non-essential details.
  • Use the three questions from Lesson 2 to justify your choices.
