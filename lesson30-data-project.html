Final Project: Collect, Process, Evaluate, Present Data

This is your chance to become a data scientist! You'll design your own complete data project — from asking a question, collecting primary data, processing it, evaluating alternatives, to presenting your findings. Everything you've learned in Lessons 21–29 comes together here.

100 minutes Collect Data Process & Evaluate Present Findings

Project Overview

Your final project brings together all the skills you've built in this unit. You'll follow the complete data workflow:

  1. Ask a clear question. What do you want to find out?
  2. Collect primary data. Use a survey, interview, observation, or measurement.
  3. Organize and process the data. Create tables, totals, averages, and percentages.
  4. Create alternatives and evaluate. Use criteria and scoring to compare options.
  5. Visualize the data. Create at least two charts (bar, pie, or line).
  6. Recommend a decision. Choose the best option, backed by evidence.
  7. Present your findings. Share your results clearly with the class.
💡
Remember: Good projects don't just show numbers — they tell a story. Your project should answer a real question and offer a useful recommendation.

Step 1 — Plan Your Project

Before you collect any data, plan carefully. Write your plan with these 5 parts:

Part What to Write Example
1. Question A clear question you want to answer "How many hours do students sleep each night?"
2. Sample Who will you collect data from, and how many? "At least 15 classmates"
3. Method Survey, interview, observation, or measurement? "Survey with 3 questions"
4. Ethics How will you protect privacy and get permission? "Anonymous, optional participation"
5. Alternatives What decision will your data support? "Recommend the ideal bedtime"
💡
Get approval first. Show your plan to your teacher before you start collecting data.
Step 2 — Collect Primary Data

Now it's time to gather your data. Remember the methods from Lesson 22:

📝

Survey

Ask many people the same clear questions. Best for numerical data.

🎤

Interview

Ask one person detailed questions. Best for explanations and opinions.

👀

Observation

Watch and record behaviour. Best for studying processes.

📏

Measurement

Use tools to get precise numbers. Best for objective data.

Collect data from at least 15 people.

Record everything in a clear table with column headings and units.

💡
Online option: Use Google Forms to collect data quickly. Responses will appear automatically in Google Sheets, where you can process them instantly.
Step 3 — Process Your Data

Once you've collected your data, process it into useful information. You must calculate:

  1. Total — Add up all your values.
    Example: Total hours of sleep across all students = 120
  2. Average — Divide the total by the number of responses.
    Example: 120 ÷ 15 = 8 hours of sleep on average
  3. Percentage — For each category, calculate part ÷ whole × 100.
    Example: 9 out of 15 students sleep 8+ hours → 60%
  4. At least one ratio — A comparison between two groups.
    Example: Ratio of students who sleep enough vs. those who don't = 9:6
Key Formulas
Average = Total ÷ Count  |  Percentage = Part ÷ Whole × 100

Step 4 — Create Alternatives and Evaluate

Use your processed data to create at least 3 alternatives and evaluate them with at least 3 criteria. Remember the scoring process from Lesson 24:

  1. Create at least 3 alternatives based on your data.
  2. Choose at least 3 criteria (cost, time, health, taste, etc.).
  3. Create a scoring table and rate each alternative from 1–5 on each criterion.
  4. Add up the scores to find the best alternative.
  5. Write one sentence justifying your choice with data.

Step 5 — Visualize Your Data

Create at least two charts to present your findings. Remember the rules from Lesson 25:

📊

Bar Chart

Compare categories. Each bar shows the value for a group.

🥧

Pie Chart

Show parts of a whole. Each slice is a percentage.

📈

Line Graph

Show change over time. Connect data points.

Every chart must include:

  • A clear title
  • Labeled axes (for bar and line charts)
  • Proper units
  • A legend if needed

Step 6 — Present Your Findings

Your presentation should last 4–5 minutes and include these 6 parts:

  1. Your question. What did you want to find out, and why?
  2. Your method. How did you collect your data, and from whom?
  3. Your processed results. Share the total, average, and percentages.
  4. Your charts. Show at least two charts and explain what they reveal.
  5. Your recommendation. State the best alternative and justify it with data.
  6. Your limitations. Be honest about what your data can and can't prove.
💡
Presentation tips: Speak clearly. Point at the chart when you explain it. Don't read from notes — explain the story behind your data.
Project Rubric

Your teacher will use this rubric to assess your project. Review it carefully before you present.

Charts 2+ correct, labeled 1–2 charts 1 chart with errors No chart

Your Turn — Plan Your Project

Use this planning sheet to organize your project before you start. Write your answers in your notebook.

Part 1 — Project Basics

  1. What is your project question?
  2. Why did you choose this topic?
  3. Who is your audience? (class, school, family, community?)

Part 2 — Data Plan

  1. What method will you use? (survey, interview, observation, measurement)
  2. Who will you collect data from? How many people?
  3. How will you keep the data anonymous?
  4. What questions will you ask (if survey)?

Part 3 — Processing Plan

  1. What totals and averages will you calculate?
  2. What percentages will you find?
  3. What alternatives will you compare?
  4. What criteria will you use to evaluate?

Part 4 — Presentation Plan

  1. What two charts will you create?
  2. How will you present your findings? (slides, poster, video, spoken)
  3. What is your recommendation based on your data?

Check Your Understanding

Answer these questions in your notebook:

  1. Why is it important to have a clear question before collecting data?
  2. Why must you get permission from people before collecting data from them?
  3. What is the difference between primary data and secondary data?
  4. Which chart type would you use to show change over time? Why?
  5. Why is it important to include at least 3 alternatives in your evaluation?
  6. What does it mean to make a recommendation backed by evidence?

Key Takeaway

Data projects are powerful tools for understanding the world and making better decisions. You've learned the full workflow: ask a question, collect data, process it, evaluate alternatives, visualize your findings, and recommend a decision. Remember: good data work is ethical, evidence-based, and always told as a story. Congratulations — you're now a junior data scientist!
