Case Study: Boiling Eggs

How do you like your eggs — soft, medium, or hard-boiled? In this lesson, you'll use real data to solve a real problem: What's the best boiling time for our school? You'll collect data, process it, create alternatives, and make a recommendation backed by evidence.

100 minutes Science + Data Real Data Recommendation
Image 1 [Hero Banner] — Three eggs in a pot of boiling water, each labeled with a different boiling time (3 min, 6 min, 10 min). A friendly illustration with a curious student scientist. Recommended size: 1000 × 400 px.
The Problem We're Solving

Imagine your school is hosting a Healthy Breakfast Day. The canteen wants to serve boiled eggs, but there's a problem: how long should they boil the eggs?

Boil them too short and they'll be runny. Boil them too long and they'll be dry and rubbery. The canteen needs to know: which boiling time will make most students happy?

💡
Our goal: Use data to recommend a boiling time that matches what most students prefer. This is how real decisions are made — with evidence, not guesses.
The Science Behind It

Why do eggs change when you boil them? It's all about protein coagulation — a fancy way of saying the egg white and yolk "set" as they heat up.

Boiling Time Result Texture
3–4 minutes Soft-boiled Runny yolk, soft white
5–6 minutes Medium-boiled Jammy yolk, firm white
8–10 minutes Hard-boiled Solid yolk, firm white

So the boiling time directly affects what the egg looks and tastes like. Now let's see what students actually prefer.

Image 2 [The Science of Boiling] — Three eggs cut in half at different boiling times. Show the yolk changing from runny to solid. Recommended size: 900 × 400 px.
Step 1 — Collect the Data

Your class surveyed students to find out how they like their eggs. Here are the results from 40 students:

Preference Number of Students Percentage
Soft-boiled 24 60%
Medium-boiled 10 25%
Hard-boiled 6 15%
Total 40 students 100%
💡
Check: The percentages add up to 100%. The counts add up to 40 — our sample size. This confirms our data is complete.

Step 3 — Evaluate the Alternatives

We could just pick the alternative with the highest preference — Alternative 1 (soft-boiled, 60%). But let's be more thorough! Let's score each alternative on 4 criteria:

📊

Data Support

How many students prefer this option?

⏱️

Time

How quickly can the canteen prepare it?

🥚

Ease

Is it easy to get consistent results?

❤️

Health

Which is healthiest overall?

Scoring each alternative (1 = poor, 5 = excellent):

Alternative Data Time Ease Health TOTAL
3 min (soft) 5 5 3 4 17
6 min (medium) 3 4 5 4 16
10 min (hard) 2 3 5 3 13
🏆
Result: Alternative 1 (soft-boiled, 3 minutes) scored the highest with 17 points. But notice — it wasn't a landslide. The difference between 3 and 6 minutes is only 1 point, so the canteen might also consider cooking both options to satisfy more students.
Image 4 [Scoring Table] — A colorful scoring table showing three alternatives compared across Data, Time, Ease, and Health criteria. Recommended size: 900 × 400 px.
Step 4 — Make a Recommendation

Now we use all our evidence to write a clear recommendation. A good recommendation has 4 parts:

  1. State the recommendation clearly.
    "We recommend that the canteen boil eggs for 3 minutes."
  2. Support it with data.
    "This matches the preference of 60% of students (24 out of 40)."
  3. Acknowledge other options.
    "However, since 25% of students prefer medium-boiled eggs, the canteen could also offer a 6-minute option."
  4. Mention limitations.
    "Our survey only included 40 students, so the results might not represent the entire school."
💡
Why mention limitations? Good scientists and data analysts are honest about what their data can and can't prove. It builds trust and shows critical thinking.

Activity — Design Your Own Case Study

Choose one of these real-world questions. Then design a mini case study:

  • What's the best time to start school in the morning?
  • What's the most popular after-school activity?
  • How much homework is the right amount each night?
  • What's the healthiest snack for the school canteen?

Your case study should include:

  1. A clear problem statement — what are you trying to decide?
  2. At least 3 alternatives to consider.
  3. At least 3 criteria for evaluating them.
  4. A scoring table with your ratings (1–5).
  5. A final recommendation with one sentence of justification.

Key Takeaway

This case study showed you how to use data to solve a real problem. You collected preferences, processed them into percentages, created alternatives, evaluated them with criteria, and made an evidence-based recommendation.
