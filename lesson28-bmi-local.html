Case Study: BMI of Local People

How healthy is your community? In this lesson, you'll become a community health researcher. You'll collect height and weight data, calculate BMI (Body Mass Index), categorize results, and use charts to analyze trends in your local area.

What is BMI?

BMI stands for Body Mass Index. It's a simple number that helps us understand whether a person's weight is healthy for their height. It's used by doctors, schools, and health organizations around the world.

BMI Formula
BMI = Weight (kg) ÷ Height² (m²)

Don't let the formula scare you! It simply means: divide the weight by the height, then divide by the height again.

💡
Example: A student who weighs 50 kg and is 1.5 m tall:
BMI = 50 ÷ (1.5 × 1.5) = 50 ÷ 2.25 = 22.2
BMI Categories

Once you calculate someone's BMI, you compare it to a range of categories. Here are the standard categories used for adults and older teens:

BMI Range Category What It Means
Below 18.5 Underweight Below healthy weight range
18.5 – 24.9 Normal Healthy weight range
25.0 – 29.9 Overweight Above healthy weight range
30.0 and above Obese Well above healthy range
💡
Important: BMI is a helpful guideline, not a perfect measure. Athletes with lots of muscle can have a high BMI but still be very healthy. BMI is just one tool — always talk to a doctor for a real health check.

Sampling & Ethics

When collecting BMI data from a community, we can't measure everyone. Instead, we take a sample — a small group that represents the larger group.

👥

Sample

A small group of people chosen to represent a larger group. Good samples are random and large enough.

🔒

Privacy

Personal data must be kept anonymous. Don't share names, photos, or individual measurements.

✋

Consent

Always ask permission before measuring someone. Explain what you're doing and why.

🤝

Respect

Never force participation. Never joke about someone's results. Health is personal.

💡
Good science starts with good ethics. Respect people's privacy and dignity at every step.

Step 2 — Calculate the BMI

Now use the BMI formula on each person. Let's walk through the first one together:

  1. Person 1 — Height 1.60 m, Weight 55 kg
    Step A: Height² = 1.60 × 1.60 = 2.56
    Step B: BMI = 55 ÷ 2.56 = 21.5 → Normal
  2. Person 2 — Height 1.75 m, Weight 85 kg
    Step A: Height² = 1.75 × 1.75 = 3.06
    Step B: BMI = 85 ÷ 3.06 = 27.8 → Overweight
  3. Person 3 — Height 1.50 m, Weight 42 kg
    Step A: Height² = 1.50 × 1.50 = 2.25
    Step B: BMI = 42 ÷ 2.25 = 18.7 → Normal

Step 4 — Visualize and Interpret

Charts make trends easier to see. Here are two charts that work well for BMI data:

📊

Bar Chart

Compares the count of people in each BMI category. Easy to see which category is largest.

🥧

Pie Chart

Shows the percentage of each category. Great for showing "parts of a whole."

📈

Line Graph

Shows BMI over time — perfect if you measure the same people again next year.

What trends do we notice?

  • Most people (60%) fall into the Normal category — a positive sign.
  • But 40% are Overweight — this is a significant trend.
  • No one was Underweight or Obese in our sample.
💡
Remember: Our sample is small (only 10 people). These trends are interesting, but we can't say they represent the whole community. A larger sample would give stronger evidence.

Step 5 — Write Your Findings

A good data scientist doesn't just show numbers — they explain what the numbers mean. Here's an example of how to write your findings:

📝
Sample findings paragraph:

"Our community sample of 10 people had an average BMI of 23.6, which falls into the Normal range. However, 40% of people were classified as Overweight, while 60% were Normal. No one was Underweight or Obese. These results suggest that while most people in our sample are at a healthy weight, a notable portion are above the healthy range. Because our sample was small, we recommend collecting more data before making strong conclusions. If the trend continues, it might be worth organizing health education activities in our community."

Notice how the paragraph:

  • Uses numbers to support claims
  • Explains what the numbers mean in plain language
  • Mentions limitations (small sample size)
  • Makes a recommendation for next steps

Activity — Design Your Own Community Study

Imagine you want to study the health of your own community (school, family, or neighborhood).

Plan your study using these questions:

  1. Research question: What do you want to find out?
  2. Sample: Who will you measure? How many people?
  3. Ethics: How will you get permission? How will you keep data anonymous?
  4. Data to collect: Height, weight, age, gender, or anything else?
  5. Analysis plan: How will you categorize and visualize the results?
  6. Limitations: What might make your data less reliable?

Bring your plan to the next lesson — we'll use it in the final project!
