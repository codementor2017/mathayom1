Testing, Edge Cases, and Improvement

Even a well-designed algorithm can break in special situations — called edge cases. In this lesson, you'll learn how to test your algorithm with tricky inputs, find errors, and improve your work like a real programmer.

What Are Edge Cases?

An edge case is a situation that is at the limit of what an algorithm can handle. It's a special case that might break the algorithm or give a wrong answer.

Edge cases help us find bugs we didn't notice before. Testing with normal cases (like 5 m × 4 m) isn't enough — we need to try tricky cases too.

💡
Why test edge cases? Because real-world problems don't always follow the rules. Testing with edge cases makes our algorithm robust — able to handle anything.
Edge Cases for the Turf Problem

Here are 6 edge cases we should test for the turf algorithm:

🎯

1. Exact Fit

Lawn area divides evenly by 0.25. Example: 5 m × 4 m = 20 m². 20 ÷ 0.25 = 80 exactly.

📐

2. Extra Space

Lawn area does not divide evenly. Example: 5.1 m × 4 m = 20.4 m². 20.4 ÷ 0.25 = 81.6 → round up to 82.

🔲

3. Odd Shape

An L-shaped lawn. Example: 6 m × 4 m minus 2 m × 2 m. Requires different calculation.

📏

4. Unit Mismatch

Lawn in cm, turf in m — or vice versa. Must convert before calculating.

🐜

5. Very Small Lawn

1 m × 1 m = 1 m². 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4 pieces. Does it still work?

🏟️

6. Very Large Lawn

100 m × 100 m = 10,000 m². 10,000 ÷ 0.25 = 40,000 pieces. Large numbers — still work?

Test 1: Exact Fit

Input: Lawn = 5 m × 4 m = 20 m²

  1. lawn_area = 5 × 4 = 20 m²
  2. piece_area = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 m²
  3. pieces = 20 ÷ 0.25 = 80
  4. ROUND UP: 80 is already a whole number → stays 80
  5. Output: 80 pieces ✅
💡
Exact fit: No rounding needed. The algorithm works perfectly here.
Test 2: Extra Space (Rounding Needed)

Input: Lawn = 5.1 m × 4 m = 20.4 m²

  1. lawn_area = 5.1 × 4 = 20.4 m²
  2. piece_area = 0.25 m²
  3. pieces = 20.4 ÷ 0.25 = 81.6
  4. ROUND UP: 81.6 → 82
  5. Output: 82 pieces ✅
💡
Rounding is essential: Without it, the algorithm would print 81.6, which is not a valid number of turf pieces.
Test 3: Unit Mismatch

Input: Lawn = 500 cm × 400 cm. Turf = 50 cm × 50 cm.

  1. lawn_area = 500 cm × 400 cm = 200,000 cm²
  2. piece_area = 50 cm × 50 cm = 2,500 cm²
  3. pieces = 200,000 ÷ 2,500 = 80
  4. ROUND UP: 80 → 80
  5. Output: 80 pieces ✅
💡
Both approaches work! Whether we use meters or centimeters, the answer is the same — as long as we're consistent with our units.

The Debugging Log

A debugging log is a table where you record each test. It helps you find and fix errors.

Test Input Expected Actual Fix
Normal 5 m × 4 m 80 80 ✅ None needed
Extra space 5.1 m × 4 m 82 81.6 ❌ Add ROUND UP
Unit mismatch 500 cm × 400 cm 80 80 ✅ None needed
Very small 1 m × 1 m 4 4 ✅ None needed
Very large 100 m × 100 m 40,000 40,000 ✅ None needed
Zero width 0 m × 5 m 0 or error 0 ✅ Maybe add warning
💡
Found a bug! Test #2 showed that we need to add the ROUND UP step. This is exactly why we test edge cases.

Fixing the Algorithm

Once we found the bug (missing ROUND UP), we need to improve the algorithm.

Before (buggy):

❌ Buggy
START
  INPUT width, length
  lawn_area = width × length
  pieces = lawn_area ÷ 0.25
  PRINT pieces
END

After (fixed):

✅ Fixed
START
  INPUT width, length
  lawn_area = width × length
  pieces = lawn_area ÷ 0.25
  ROUND UP pieces ← added
  PRINT pieces
END
💡
Debugging is a normal part of programming. Even professional programmers test their code many times before it works perfectly.

Activity — Peer Test — Try to Break It!

Now it's time for a challenge: exchange algorithms with another group and try to break theirs with a new edge case.

Your mission:

  1. Read the other group's algorithm carefully.
  2. Come up with one new edge case they haven't tested.
  3. Run their algorithm on your edge case.
  4. Does it work? Or does it break?
  5. If it breaks, tell them how to fix it.

Ideas for edge cases to try:

  • A lawn with a very long but narrow shape (like 50 m × 0.5 m)
  • A lawn measured in millimeters
  • A very tiny lawn (0.5 m × 0.5 m)
  • A lawn with a hole (donut shape)
  • Lawn with decimal dimensions (3.75 m × 4.25 m)
💡
Why do we do this? Because finding bugs is how we make algorithms stronger. When someone breaks your algorithm, they're helping you improve it.

Key Takeaway

Testing with edge cases is how we find bugs and improve our algorithms. Use a debugging log to record what you tested, what you expected, what actually happened, and how you fixed it. Remember: even the best algorithms need to be tested with normal cases, boundary cases, and unexpected inputs. Debugging isn't failure — it's a normal part of building great solutions.
