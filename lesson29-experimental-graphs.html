Graphs from Experiments and Trends

Science is all about experiments — changing one thing and measuring what happens. In this lesson, you'll learn how to record experimental data, turn it into a line graph, and use that graph to spot trends and make predictions about the future.

What is a Trend?

A trend is a pattern in data over time. When you look at a line graph, you can quickly see whether something is:

  • Going up — increasing
  • Going down — decreasing
  • Staying the same — flat/stable
  • Changing fast or slowly — steep or gentle slope
💡
Key idea: Line graphs are the best tool for showing trends because they connect data points over time, revealing the shape of change.
Experiments and Variables

A good experiment changes only one thing at a time. We call these "variables." Here are the three types:

🔄

Independent Variable

What you change on purpose. Example: the time you leave the water to cool (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 minutes).

📏

Dependent Variable

What you measure. It "depends" on the independent variable. Example: the temperature of the water.

🔒

Control Variable

What stays the same. Example: the amount of water, the starting temperature, the type of container.

📊

Result

The data you collect. These become the points on your line graph.

Example Experiment — Cooling Water

Let's look at a simple experiment: How does water temperature change as it cools?

We boil water to 100°C, then measure its temperature every 2 minutes for 10 minutes.

Our variables:

  • Independent: Time (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 minutes)
  • Dependent: Water temperature (°C)
  • Control: Same amount of water, same container, same room temperature

Our data:

Notice: The temperature drops quickly at first (100 → 85 = 15°C drop) and then more slowly (55 → 50 = 5°C drop). The trend is not linear — it curves.

How to Make a Line Graph

Follow these 6 steps to turn your experimental data into a clear line graph:

  1. Draw the axes.
    The x-axis (horizontal) usually shows time. The y-axis (vertical) shows the measurement.
  2. Label the axes.
    Write what each axis shows, and include units (minutes, °C, cm, etc.).
  3. Choose the scale.
    Make sure your numbers fit evenly on the axis. Example: 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100.
  4. Plot the points.
    For each pair of values, place a dot at the correct position.
  5. Connect the dots.
    Draw a smooth line (or straight segments) through the points. Don't connect them with a zig-zag.
  6. Add a title.
    Give the graph a clear, descriptive title like "Water Temperature Over Time."
Analyzing Trends

Once you have a line graph, you can describe the trend in words. Here's how:

Trend Shape How to Describe It Example
Steady rise "Increasing steadily" Plant growing taller each week
Steady fall "Decreasing steadily" Water temperature cooling
Fast then slow "Rapid change, then slowing down" Water cooling quickly at first
Slow then fast "Slow start, then accelerating" Bacteria growth in a culture
Flat "Staying the same" Temperature of ice melting (0°C)
Going up and down "Fluctuating" or "oscillating" Pendulum swing height

Making Predictions

Once you've identified a trend, you can predict what happens next. Here's how:

  1. Look at the pattern.
    Is the temperature falling by a consistent amount? Or is the rate slowing?
  2. Extend the graph.
    If the water cools 5°C every 2 minutes, what will the temperature be at 12 minutes?
  3. Make a clear statement.
    "We predict the temperature will be about 47°C at 12 minutes."
  4. Explain your reasoning.
    "Because the cooling rate has been slowing, we expect the next drop to be smaller than previous ones."
💡
Remember: Predictions are educated guesses, not certainties. That's why scientists always say "we predict" or "we expect" — not "it will definitely happen."

Activity — Conduct Your Own Experiment

Choose one of these simple experiments (or come up with your own):

  • Measure how quickly water cools in a cup (every 2 minutes for 10 minutes).
  • Measure the length of a rubber band when different weights are hung on it.
  • Measure the temperature of ice melting (every minute until it's fully melted).
  • Count how many times a pendulum swings in 15 seconds, with different string lengths.

Follow these steps:

  1. Identify your independent, dependent, and control variables.
  2. Collect at least 6 data points.
  3. Create a data table with units.
  4. Draw a line graph with a title, labels, and units.
  5. Describe the trend in one sentence.
  6. Make a prediction about what happens next.

Key Takeaway

Experiments produce data over time, and line graphs are the best way to show trends. Remember the three variables: independent (what you change), dependent (what you measure), and control (what stays the same). When you analyze a graph, describe the shape of the trend and use it to make predictions — always explaining your reasoning. Next lesson, you'll bring everything together in your final project!
