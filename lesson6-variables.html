Variables, Constants, and Units

In every problem, some things change and some things stay the same. Knowing the difference is a superpower! In this lesson, you'll learn about variables, constants, and units — three tools that help you solve problems accurately.

100 minutes Variables Constants Units
Image 1 [Hero Banner] — A growing plant with a height ruler next to it. The height changes (variable) but the type of plant stays the same (constant). Friendly, science-y illustration. Recommended size: 1000 × 400 px.
Warm-Up: What Changes?

Look at a growing plant. Over time, what changes? And what stays the same?

📏

What CHANGES

  • The plant's height
  • The number of leaves
  • The color of the leaves
  • The size of the stem
🔒

What STAYS THE SAME

  • The type of plant
  • The pot it grows in
  • The species (e.g., tomato, sunflower)
💡
Key idea: Things that change are called variables. Things that stay the same are called constants.
What is a Variable?

A variable is a value that can change from problem to problem or over time. In math, we often use letters like x or y to represent variables.

Examples of variables:

  • The length of a lawn (could be 4 m, 6 m, or 10 m)
  • The number of students in a class (could be 25, 30, or 35)
  • The weight of a package (could be 2 kg, 5 kg, or 10 kg)
  • The time a car travels (could be 1 hour, 2 hours, or 5 hours)
Variable Notation
Length = L  |  Width = W  |  Time = t  |  Area = A
What is a Constant?

A constant is a value that stays the same in a given problem. Even if the problem changes, the constant stays fixed.

Examples of constants:

  • The size of one turf piece (50 cm × 50 cm) — always the same
  • The value of π (pi) — always 3.14159…
  • The speed of light — always 300,000,000 m/s
  • The number of days in a week — always 7
Constant Notation
π ≈ 3.14  |  Turf piece = 50 cm × 50 cm  |  Days per week = 7

Real-World Examples

Let's look at three problems and identify the variables and constants in each:

Problem Variable Constant
Buying paint for a wall Wall area (changes with each room) Paint coverage per can (fixed)
Packing books into boxes Number of books (changes) Box size (fixed)
Laying turf in a lawn Lawn size (changes) Turf piece size — 50 cm × 50 cm (fixed)
Filling a water tank Tank size (changes) Water flow rate (fixed)
💡
Key idea: In every problem, the variable is what you're solving for, and the constant is what you already know.
Why Units Matter

A number without a unit is meaningless. If I say "The lawn is 5" — 5 what? 5 cm? 5 m? 5 km? Each one is a very different lawn!

Units tell us what we're measuring. Common units:

📏

Length

Millimeters (mm), centimeters (cm), meters (m), kilometers (km)

🟦

Area

Square centimeters (cm²), square meters (m²), square kilometers (km²)

⚖️

Weight / Mass

Grams (g), kilograms (kg), tons (t)

⏱️

Time

Seconds (s), minutes (min), hours (h), days

💡
Always include units in your answers. "The area is 20 m²" is much clearer than "The area is 20."
Image 3 [Units Matter] — Three visuals showing the difference between 5 cm, 5 m, and 5 km. The same number means very different things depending on the unit! Recommended size: 900 × 400 px.
Unit Conversions

Sometimes you need to convert from one unit to another. Here are the most common conversions:

Length
1 m = 100 cm  |  1 km = 1000 m
Area
1 m² = 10,000 cm²
Volume
1 L = 1000 mL

Example conversion:

  • 50 cm = 0.5 m (divide by 100)
  • 2.5 m = 250 cm (multiply by 100)
  • 1 m² = 10,000 cm²
💡
Golden rule: When solving a problem, all measurements must be in the same unit. Convert first, then calculate.
Guided Practice: Identify and Convert

Let's work through a problem together: "Laying turf in a lawn."

The problem: A lawn is 5 m long and 4 m wide. Each turf piece is 50 cm × 50 cm. How many pieces do we need?

  1. Identify variables.
    The lawn size (5 m × 4 m) — it can change if we have a different lawn.
  2. Identify constants.
    The turf piece size (50 cm × 50 cm) — every piece is the same.
  3. Check the units.
    The lawn is in meters. The turf is in centimeters. We need to convert!
  4. Convert units.
    50 cm = 0.5 m. So one turf piece is 0.5 m × 0.5 m.
  5. Now the units match — we can do the math.
💡
Notice: We ignored non-essential details like the color of the grass, the brand of the turf, and the weather. Abstraction in action!

Key Takeaway

Every problem has variables (what changes) and constants (what stays the same). Always include units when writing measurements — a number without a unit is meaningless. When solving problems, remember to convert all measurements to the same unit before calculating. Next lesson, you'll use everything you've learned to solve a real turf-laying problem step by step!

Homework

Part 1 — Measure at home:

  • Find three objects at home and measure them (with permission).
  • Record each measurement with its unit (cm, m, kg, etc.).
  • For example: the length of your bed, the width of your table, the height of your door.

Part 2 — Convert one measurement:

  • Choose one of your measurements.
  • Convert it to a different unit (for example, cm to m).
  • Show your work.

Part 3 — Think about variables and constants:

  • Find one real-life situation with variables and constants (cooking, shopping, etc.).
  • Write down 2 variables and 2 constants.
  • Explain why each is a variable or a constant.
