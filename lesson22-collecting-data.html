Methods of Collecting Primary Data

Now that you know what primary data is, it's time to learn how to collect it. In this lesson, you'll explore four powerful methods — surveys, interviews, observations, and measurements — and learn how to design good questions that give you useful data.

Why Do Collection Methods Matter?

Imagine you want to know how many hours your classmates sleep each night. You could:

  • Ask them to write a number on a piece of paper — survey
  • Ask one friend detailed questions about their sleep — interview
  • Watch how long they sleep — observation (not practical!)
  • Measure their sleep with a device — measurement

Each method gives you different types of data. Choosing the right method depends on your goal.

💡
Key idea: The best method is the one that gives you the most reliable data for your specific question.
The Four Methods

Here's a closer look at each method — when to use it and what kind of data it gives you.

📝

Survey

Ask many people the same questions. Great for getting lots of responses quickly. Best for numerical or yes/no answers.

🎤

Interview

Ask one person in depth. Great for detailed answers, explanations, and opinions you can't get from a survey.

👀

Observation

Watch and record what happens. Great for studying behaviour and processes that people can't easily report.

📏

Measurement

Use tools (ruler, scale, thermometer, stopwatch) to get precise numbers. Best for objective, numerical data.

Designing Good Survey Questions

A survey is only as good as its questions. If your questions are confusing or unclear, your data will be useless. Here are the 5 rules for great survey questions:

  1. Be clear — Use simple words everyone understands.
  2. One idea per question — Don't ask two things at once.
  3. Avoid leading questions — Don't push people to answer a certain way.
  4. Include units — "How many hours?" not just "How much?"
  5. Give clear options — Especially for multiple choice.
Good vs Bad Questions

Let's compare. The good question gives clear, useful data. The bad question gives confusing or useless data.

✅ Good Question

"How many eggs do you eat per week?"

Clear, uses units (eggs/week), and gives a number you can average. Why it's good: One idea, easy to answer, gives numerical data.

❌ Bad Question

"Do you like eggs?"

Only gives yes/no, doesn't tell you how many or why. Why it's bad: Too vague, no useful number, doesn't measure behaviour.

✅ Good Question

"How many minutes do you usually boil an egg?"

Clear unit (minutes), specific action (boil an egg), gives a number you can average.

❌ Bad Question

"Don't you think eggs are the best breakfast?"

Leading question — it pushes people to agree. Not objective, gives biased data.

💡
Test: Read your question out loud. If it's confusing to you, it will be confusing to your survey takers too!

Recording Your Data Accurately

Once you've collected your data, you need to record it clearly. Otherwise, you'll forget what you found or make mistakes.

Tips for accurate recording:

  • Use a table with clear column headings.
  • Write the units next to your numbers (kg, cm, minutes, etc.).
  • Record the data immediately — don't rely on memory.
  • Double-check your numbers.
  • Note the date and source of your data.

Activity — Design Your Own Survey

Your task is to design a survey about egg consumption in your school. Write 5 clear survey questions.

Your 5 questions should include:

  • At least 2 questions with numbers (like "How many…")
  • At least 1 multiple choice question
  • At least 1 question that gives categories (like soft-boiled, hard-boiled)

Example questions you could use:

  1. How many eggs do you eat per week?
  2. How do you usually eat eggs? (boiled, fried, scrambled, other)
  3. How long do you boil an egg? (minutes)
  4. Do you prefer soft-boiled or hard-boiled eggs?
  5. Would you change your habit if you knew the perfect boiling time?

  5. Write one bad survey question and explain how to fix it.

Key Takeaway

The four methods for collecting primary data are surveys, interviews, observations, and measurements. Each one is best for a different type of question. When you design a survey, remember the rules: be clear, use units, ask one thing at a time, and avoid leading questions. Next lesson, you'll learn how to organize and process the data you collect!
