Introduction to Data and Primary Sources

Every day, you see data all around you — the weather, your test scores, the price of snacks, your heart rate. But where does all this data come from? In this lesson, you'll learn what data is, what makes it primary or secondary, and how to collect your own data from the real world.

What is Data?

Data is a collection of raw facts and numbers. On their own, they don't mean much. For example: 5, 10, 15, 20. What do these numbers tell you? Not much — until you know what they represent.

Information is what you get when you process data and give it meaning. For example: "The average score of the class is 15." Now the numbers mean something!

💡
Remember: Data is raw. Information is processed. Data → Process → Information.
Primary vs Secondary Data

Data comes from different places. The most important distinction is who collected it.

👤

Primary Data

Data you collect yourself. You control how it's collected, so it's reliable for your own problem.

🌍

Secondary Data

Data someone else collected. You find it in books, websites, reports, or articles.

Point of Comparison Primary Data Secondary Data
Who collects it? You (the student) Someone else
How is it collected? Survey, measurement, observation, interview Reading books, websites, reports
Examples Measuring your classmate's height; counting cars Reading average height online; watching a video
Reliability for your problem High — you control the method Depends on the original source

Key Vocabulary

Here are the words you'll use throughout this lesson. Read them carefully and try to use them when you answer questions.

  • Data — raw facts and numbers (e.g., 5, 10, 15).
  • Information — processed data with meaning (e.g., average = 10).
  • Primary data — data collected directly by you.
  • Secondary data — data collected by someone else.
  • Source — where the data comes from.
  • Collect — to gather data.
  • Survey — asking many people the same questions.
  • Observation — watching and recording what you see.
How to Collect Primary Data

There are four main methods for collecting primary data. Each one is useful for different situations.

📝

Survey

Ask many people the same questions. Great for getting lots of responses quickly.

🎤

Interview

Ask one person in depth. Great for getting detailed answers and explanations.

👀

Observation

Watch and record what happens. Great for studying behaviour and processes.

📏

Measurement

Use tools to get numbers. Great for getting precise, numerical data.

💡
Tip: When you design a survey question, keep it clear and one idea per question. For example, "How many eggs do you eat per week?" is a good question. "Do you like eggs?" only gives a yes/no answer.

Your Turn — Classify These Sources

Read each example below. Decide whether it is Primary data or Secondary data. Then write your answer in your notebook.

  1. Asking your classmates about their favourite fruit. (Primary / Secondary)
  2. Reading a newspaper article about rainfall. (Primary / Secondary)
  3. Measuring the temperature in the classroom. (Primary / Secondary)
  4. Watching a YouTube video about BMI. (Primary / Secondary)
  5. Interviewing the school nurse. (Primary / Secondary)
  6. Reading a table from a textbook. (Primary / Secondary)
  7. Surveying your parents about egg consumption. (Primary / Secondary)
  8. Checking the school website for exam results. (Primary / Secondary)
  9. Counting cars passing the school gate. (Primary / Secondary)
  10. Reading a government report about population. (Primary / Secondary)
💡
Hint: If you collected it yourself, it's primary. If someone else collected it first, it's secondary.

Activity — Plan Your Own Data Collection

Choose one of the questions below. Then design a simple plan to collect primary data for it.

  • How many hours do students sleep each night?
  • How many eggs does your family eat per week?
  • What is the average height of students in this class?
  • How much water do students drink per day?

Write your plan using these four parts:

  1. Question: What do you want to find out?
  2. Source: Who or what will you collect data from?
  3. Method: Survey, interview, observation, or measurement?
  4. Data to record: What exactly will you write down?

Check Your Understanding

Answer these questions in your notebook to test what you've learned:

  1. What is the difference between data and information?
  2. Give one example of primary data and one example of secondary data.
  3. Why is primary data more reliable for solving your own problem?
  4. Name two methods for collecting primary data.
  5. Write one good survey question and one bad survey question. Explain why.

Key Takeaway

Data is all around us. When you collect it yourself, it becomes primary data — the most reliable kind for solving your own problems. Remember the four collection methods: survey, interview, observation, and measurement. Next lesson, you'll learn how to put these methods into action!
