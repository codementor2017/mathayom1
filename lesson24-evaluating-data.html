Creating Alternatives and Evaluating Data

Now that you have processed data, how do you use it to make a decision? In this lesson, you'll learn how to create alternatives, evaluate them using criteria, and choose the best option based on evidence.

100 minutes Create Alternatives Evaluate with Criteria Make a Decision

What Are Alternatives?

An alternative is simply a different option you could choose. Almost every decision has more than one option!

Think about breakfast. You could have:

  • Eggs and toast
  • Cereal with milk
  • Fruit and yogurt

Each is an alternative. But how do you decide which is best? You need to evaluate them — compare them using criteria (things that matter to you).

💡
Key idea: Alternatives + Criteria + Evidence = Better decisions. Data helps us evaluate alternatives objectively, not just by feelings.

Evaluation Criteria — How Do We Choose?

To choose the best alternative, you need criteria. Criteria are the things that matter when making a decision.

Here are some common criteria you might use:

Cost Time Health Taste Availability Data Support Quality Environmental Impact
💡
Tip: Not every criterion matters equally. For a school project, you might care more about cost and time. For a family meal, you might care more about taste and health.
Evaluating Alternatives — Scoring Table

One way to evaluate alternatives is to score each one on each criterion. Use a scale like 1–5 (1 = poor, 5 = excellent).

Example: Which boiling time is best for our school canteen?

Alternative Cost Time Health Taste TOTAL
3-minute (soft) 5 5 4 5 19
6-minute (medium) 4 4 4 3 15
10-minute (hard) 3 3 4 2 12
🏆
Result: The 3-minute soft-boiled egg scored highest (19 points). This matches the survey data — most students prefer soft-boiled!

How to Make an Evidence-Based Decision

Follow these 5 steps to make a decision that's backed by evidence, not just feelings:

  1. State the problem clearly: What decision do you need to make?
    Example: "What boiling time should the school canteen use?"
  2. Create at least 3 alternatives: List different possible options.
    Example: 3-minute, 6-minute, or 10-minute boiling times.
  3. Choose your criteria: What matters most?
    Example: cost, time, health, taste.
  4. Score each alternative: Use your processed data and criteria to rate each option.
    Example: give each one a score from 1–5 on each criterion.
  5. Choose the winner and justify it: Pick the highest-scoring option and explain why using your data.
    Example: "We chose 3 minutes because 60% of students preferred soft-boiled eggs, and it scored the highest overall."

Activity — Evaluate Your Own Data

Use the processed data from your Lesson 23 homework (your survey results).

Complete these tasks:

  1. Create 3 alternatives based on your data.
  2. Choose at least 3 criteria for evaluating them.
  3. Create a scoring table and rate each alternative (1–5) on each criterion.
  4. Choose the best alternative based on the scores.
  5. Write one sentence justifying your choice using your data.

Bring your evaluation to the next lesson — you'll use it in the final project!

Check Your Understanding

Answer these questions in your notebook:

  1. What is an alternative? Give one example from your own life.
  2. What is a criterion? Name three criteria you might use to evaluate a food choice.
  3. Why is it important to use data when evaluating alternatives?
  4. What is the difference between a subjective choice (based on feelings) and an objective choice (based on evidence)?
  5. Describe the 5 steps for making an evidence-based decision.
  6. Why should you create at least 3 alternatives instead of just 2?

Key Takeaway

Good decisions don't happen by accident. Create alternatives, choose criteria, score each option, and choose the best one using evidence. This is how data helps us make smarter decisions in school, at home, and in life. Next lesson, you'll learn how to present your findings visually with charts and graphs!
