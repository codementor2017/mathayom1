Evaluating the Appropriateness of Content

Not everything online is good for you — or good for others. In this lesson, you'll learn how to judge what's appropriate and what isn't. You'll explore privacy violations, rude criticism, and harmful content — and discover what to do when you see something wrong online.

100 minutes Appropriate vs Not Privacy & Respect Report & Respond

What Does "Appropriate" Mean?

When we say content is appropriate, we mean it's respectful, safe, and suitable for the situation. When it's inappropriate, it might hurt someone, break the rules, or just be wrong for the place where it's shared.

Let's compare:

✅

Appropriate Content

Congratulating a friend on their post. Sharing educational videos. Asking a respectful question.

❌

Inappropriate Content

Making fun of someone's appearance. Sharing someone's private photo without permission. Posting mean comments.

💡
Key idea: Appropriate content respects others and follows the rules of the platform. Inappropriate content breaks one or both of these.
Four Types of Inappropriate Content

When you're online, you might see four main types of inappropriate content. Let's look at each one:

🔒

1. Privacy Violation

Sharing someone's personal information or private photos without their permission.

Example: Posting a friend's address without asking.

🚫

2. Obscenity

Sexual, violent, or offensive material that isn't suitable for all audiences.

Example: Posting inappropriate images in a school group chat.

💢

3. Rude Criticism

Insulting, mocking, or humiliating others — even if meant as a "joke."

Example: Leaving a mean comment about someone's photo.

😢

4. Cyberbullying

Repeated harmful behavior online, meant to hurt or scare someone.

Example: Sending mean messages to a classmate every day.

Why Does It Matter? The Impact

Inappropriate content isn't just "rude" — it has real consequences for the people involved. Here's what can happen:

Who Is Affected How They're Affected
The Target Emotional harm, anxiety, depression, fear, lowered self-esteem
The Person Posting Damaged reputation, loss of trust, school discipline, future consequences
Friends & Family Worry, embarrassment, conflict, broken relationships
The Community A toxic online culture, more fear, less trust among peers
The Poster's Future Colleges, employers, and future friends can see it — forever
💡
Remember: Online actions have real-world consequences. A single post can affect someone for years — or affect your own future. Think twice before you post.

The Three-Question Test

Before you post, share, or react to something online, ask yourself these three simple questions:

  1. Is it respectful?
    Would you say this to the person's face? Would you want it said about you?
  2. Is it true?
    Are you sharing facts or rumors? Is it accurate?
  3. Is it helpful?
    Does it add something positive, or does it just hurt, embarrass, or stir up trouble?
💡
If the answer to any of these is "no" — don't post it. This simple test prevents most bad online decisions.
How to Respond to Inappropriate Content

What should you do if you see something inappropriate online? Follow these 6 steps:

  1. Don't share or like it.
    Sharing or liking a bad post makes it spread faster and shows you agree with it.
  2. Don't argue publicly.
    Responding angrily in the comments often makes things worse. Stay calm.
  3. Support the target privately.
    Send a kind message to the person being hurt. Let them know they're not alone.
  4. Screenshot the evidence.
    Before reporting, save a screenshot. It's proof if you need it later.
  5. Report to the platform.
    Use the "Report" button. Most platforms have rules against harassment and privacy violations.
  6. Tell a trusted adult.
    If someone is being hurt badly, tell a parent, teacher, or school counselor. Don't try to handle it alone.

Your Turn — Appropriate or Inappropriate?

Read each of these 10 scenarios. Decide: Appropriate or Inappropriate? If inappropriate, name the type (Privacy, Obscenity, Rude Criticism, or Cyberbullying) and explain why in your notebook.

  1. Posting a funny video of a friend falling down — without asking.
  2. Sharing a classmate's test score without permission.
  3. Writing a mean comment on someone's photo.
  4. Sharing educational content about science.
  5. Posting a photo of someone without asking.
  6. Using offensive language in a class group chat.
  7. Complimenting someone's artwork.
  8. Sharing a rumor about a teacher.
  9. Reporting a harmful post to the platform.
  10. Sending a threatening message to someone.
💡
Use the Three-Question Test: Is it respectful? Is it true? Is it helpful?

Activity — Create a Response Plan

Work in a group of 3–4 students. Choose one of the scenarios below and create a response plan. Present it to the class.

😢

Scenario A

A classmate is being cyberbullied in a group chat.

📸

Scenario B

Someone posted your photo without your permission.

😠

Scenario C

A friend shared offensive content online.

🗣️

Scenario D

You see a rumor about a teacher spreading online.

Your response plan should include:

  1. Is this appropriate or inappropriate? Why?
  2. Who is affected? (Target, poster, friends, school, community)
  3. What should you do? (List at least 4 actions.)
  4. What should the school do? (If applicable.)
  5. What could prevent this in the future?

Create a poster, a slide, or a short skit to present your plan.

Check Your Understanding

Answer these questions in your notebook:

  1. What does "appropriate content" mean?
  2. Name the four types of inappropriate content, with a short definition of each.
  3. Why is it harmful to share someone's photo without permission?
  4. What are the three questions in the Three-Question Test?
  5. Name four steps for responding to inappropriate content.
  6. Why is it important to tell a trusted adult if someone is being badly hurt online?

Key Takeaway

Good digital citizens evaluate content carefully. Before you post, ask: Is it respectful? Is it true? Is it helpful? When you see inappropriate content, don't share it, support the target, report it, and tell a trusted adult. Your choices online shape your community — make them count. Next lesson, you'll dive deeper into cyberbullying and respectful online communication!

Homework

Part 1 — Content evaluation practice:

  • Look at your own social media feed or the feed of a family member (with permission).
  • Find three posts and evaluate each one:
    • Is it appropriate? Why or why not?
    • Does it pass the Three-Question Test?
  • Write your analysis in your notebook.

Part 2 — Write a personal pledge:

  • Write a short pledge: "I will be respectful online by…"
  • Include at least three specific promises you're making to yourself.
  • Sign and date it.
