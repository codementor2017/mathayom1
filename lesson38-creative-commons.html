Creative Commons and Using Media Legally

Can I use this photo in my project? Can I add this song to my video? The answer isn't always "yes" or "no" — sometimes it's "yes, if you follow the rules." In this lesson, you'll learn about Creative Commons, the six main licenses, and how to give proper attribution to the creators whose work you use.

Can I Use This?

Imagine you find the perfect photo online for a school project. It's beautiful. It's exactly what you need. You want to use it.

But here's the question: Can you use it?

The answer is: it depends. Not everything online is free to use. Most of it belongs to someone — an artist, a photographer, a writer, a musician. They created it, and they have the right to say how it's used.

💡
Key idea: The person who creates something owns it. This is called copyright. You can't just take it and use it however you want.
What is Copyright?

Copyright is the legal ownership of a creative work. When someone writes, draws, photographs, composes, or films something original, they automatically own the copyright to it.

Copyright covers things like:

  • Photos and images
  • Music and songs
  • Videos and movies
  • Books and articles
  • Artwork and illustrations
  • Software and code
💡
Default rule: Unless the creator says otherwise, you cannot copy, share, modify, or use the work without permission. That's why licenses exist — to give permission in advance.
What is Creative Commons?

Creative Commons (CC) is a non-profit organization that created a set of free, easy-to-understand licenses (legal permissions). Creators can use these licenses to tell the world: "You can use my work — here are the rules."

Think of a CC license as a permission slip from the creator. It saves everyone time and avoids legal problems.

🆓

Free to Use

CC works are free to use, share, and sometimes modify — as long as you follow the rules.

🌍

Global Standard

CC licenses are recognized worldwide. You can use CC work from any country.

🤝

Gives Credit

Most CC licenses require you to credit the creator (called attribution).

🎨

Encourages Sharing

CC lets creators share their work with the world while still keeping some rights.

The Six CC Licenses

There are six main Creative Commons licenses. Each one gives different permissions. Let's look at each one, from the most open to the most restricted:

License What You CAN Do What You CAN'T Do
CC BY
Attribution		 Use, share, modify — even commercially Forget to credit the creator
CC BY-SA
Attribution + Share-Alike		 Use, share, modify — if you share under the same license Use a more restrictive license on your version
CC BY-ND
Attribution + No Derivatives		 Use and share the work as-is Modify or remix the work
CC BY-NC
Attribution + Non-Commercial		 Use, share, modify for non-commercial purposes Use for commercial purposes (making money)
CC BY-NC-SA
Attribution + Non-Commercial + Share-Alike		 Use, share, modify — for non-commercial use, and share under the same license Use commercially or with a different license
CC BY-NC-ND
Attribution + Non-Commercial + No Derivatives		 Share the work as-is, for non-commercial purposes Modify it or use it commercially
💡
Also: CC0 (Public Domain). When a creator uses CC0, they give up all rights. You can use the work however you want — no attribution required (but it's still polite!).
Image 3 [Six CC Licenses] — Six colorful license cards, each showing one of the six CC licenses with its abbreviation and symbols. Recommended size: 900 × 400 px.
The Four CC Symbols

Every CC license is built from a combination of four symbols. Learning them helps you understand any license at a glance:

👤

BY = Attribution

You must credit the creator. This is in every CC license (except CC0).

🔄

SA = Share-Alike

If you modify the work, you must share your version under the same license.

🚫

NC = Non-Commercial

You cannot use the work to make money. Personal and school use are usually OK.

🔒

ND = No Derivatives

You cannot modify or remix the work. Use it only as-is.

💡
How to read a CC license: Read the letters after "CC." For example, "CC BY-NC-SA" means Attribution + Non-Commercial + Share-Alike.
Image 4 [Four CC Symbols] — Four circular icons, each showing one CC symbol with a short label underneath. Recommended size: 900 × 350 px.
How to Give Attribution

Attribution means giving credit to the creator. Every CC license (except CC0) requires it. A proper attribution includes four pieces of information:

  1. Title — What is the work called?
  2. Author — Who created it?
  3. Source — Where can we find it? (Usually a link.)
  4. License — Which CC license does it use? (Include a link.)

Example attribution:

📝
"Sunset Over the Mountains" by Jane Doe, from Unsplash.com, licensed under CC BY 4.0.
💡
Remember the "TASL" rule: Title, Author, Source, License. That's the order to write them in.
Where to Find CC Media

Where can you find images, music, and videos with CC licenses? Here are some trusted sources:

📷

Photos

Unsplash, Pexels, Pixabay, and Flickr (search "CC")

🎵

Music

Free Music Archive, Incompetech, YouTube Audio Library

🎥

Videos

Vimeo, Pexels Videos, Coverr

🎨

Illustrations

Wikimedia Commons, OpenClipArt, Undraw

💡
Always verify the license before using any work. Some sites show CC symbols on each image; others have a separate license page.

Your Turn — License Match

For each of these 6 situations, decide which CC license is required or allowed. Write your answer in your notebook.

  1. You want to use a photo in a school project and make no money from it.
  2. You want to modify a song and share your new version under the same license.
  3. You want to use a photo in a book you plan to sell.
  4. You want to use a work but don't want to credit the creator.
  5. You want to use a photo in a YouTube video where you make money from ads.
  6. You want to remix a video but keep the original message unchanged.
💡
Hint: Read each license's permissions carefully. Remember: NC = no money, ND = no changes, SA = share the same way.

Activity — Create a CC Attribution Page

Work in a group of 3–4 students. Choose a topic (e.g., environment, technology, health, sports). Then:

  1. Find 3 images with CC licenses related to your topic (from Unsplash, Pexels, Pixabay, etc.).
  2. Download each image (or save the link).
  3. Create an attribution page in Google Docs, Slides, or a poster.
  4. For each image, include:
    • The image itself
    • Title
    • Author
    • Source (link)
    • License (with a link)
  5. Present your attribution page to the class.

Bonus: If you want, use one of the images in a small poster about your topic — with the attribution displayed nearby.

Check Your Understanding

Answer these questions in your notebook:

  1. What is copyright? Who owns the copyright of a work?
  2. What is Creative Commons? Why was it created?
  3. Name the four CC symbols and what each one means.
  4. What are the four pieces of information in a proper attribution? (Hint: TASL.)
  5. What does CC0 (Public Domain) mean?
  6. Why is it important to use CC-licensed work correctly?

Key Takeaway

Creative Commons lets creators share their work legally and freely. The six CC licenses give different permissions — from the open CC BY to the restricted CC BY-NC-ND. When you use CC work, always give attribution with the TASL rule (Title, Author, Source, License). Using media legally is a mark of respect — for creators and for yourself.
