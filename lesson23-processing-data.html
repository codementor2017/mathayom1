Organizing and Processing Data

You've collected your data — now what? Raw numbers on a page don't tell you much on their own. In this lesson, you'll learn how to organize data into tables and process it to find totals, averages, and percentages that reveal the real story.

100 minutes Frequency Tables Totals & Averages Percentages

Why Do We Process Data?

Imagine you collected these numbers from a survey asking classmates how many eggs they eat per week:

5, 3, 4, 6, 5, 2, 4, 5, 6, 3

Looking at these numbers, what do you actually learn? Not much at a glance. But if you process them, you can find:

  • The total (how many eggs everyone eats altogether)
  • The average (how many eggs a typical student eats)
  • The percentage (what fraction of students eat more than 4 eggs)

Processing turns raw data into useful information that helps us answer questions and make decisions.

💡
Remember: Data → Process → Information. Processing is what gives data meaning.
Step 1: Organize Your Data

The first step in processing is to organize your data into a clear table. A well-organized table makes it easy to see patterns and do calculations.

The simplest way is a frequency table. "Frequency" just means "how many times each value appears."

Example: Egg consumption survey results

Values from 10 students: 5, 3, 4, 6, 5, 2, 4, 5, 6, 3

Value (eggs per week) Frequency (how many students)
21 student
32 students
42 students
53 students
62 students
📋
Check: The frequency column should add up to your total number of responses. Here: 1 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 2 = 10 students. ✅

Step 2: Process the Data

Once your data is organized, you can process it in three main ways: totals, averages, and percentages.

➕

Total

The sum of all values. Add every number together to find the total.

⚖️

Average (Mean)

The typical value. Divide the total by the number of values.

📊

Percentage

A ratio out of 100. Part ÷ whole × 100.

⚖️

Ratio

A comparison between two amounts. Part ÷ whole.

Formula
Average = Total ÷ Number of Values
Formula
Percentage = Part ÷ Whole × 100

Worked Example — Let's Process!

Using the egg survey data (5, 3, 4, 6, 5, 2, 4, 5, 6, 3), let's walk through all three processing methods.

  1. Find the Total: Add all values together.
    5 + 3 + 4 + 6 + 5 + 2 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 3 = 43 eggs
  2. Count the Values: How many numbers do we have?
    There are 10 students (10 numbers total).
  3. Find the Average: Total ÷ Number of Values.
    43 ÷ 10 = 4.3 eggs per student
  4. Find a Percentage: How many students eat more than 4 eggs?
    Values > 4: 5, 6, 5, 5, 6 = 5 students
    Percentage = 5 ÷ 10 × 100 = 50%
  5. Interpret Your Results: What does the data tell you?
    "On average, students eat about 4.3 eggs per week, and half of them (50%) eat more than 4 eggs per week."
💡
Always write the meaning of your numbers, not just the numbers themselves. "The average is 4.3" is meaningless without "…eggs per student per week."
Processing with a Spreadsheet

You can do all these calculations by hand, but spreadsheets (like Google Sheets or Excel) can do them for you in seconds. Here are the key formulas:

What You Want Spreadsheet Formula
Total (sum) =SUM(B2:B11)
Average (mean) =AVERAGE(B2:B11)
Count of items =COUNT(B2:B11)
Percentage =B2/$B$12*100
💡
Tip: The $ sign in $B$12 "locks" the cell so that when you copy the formula down, it keeps referencing the same total cell.

Your Turn — Process This Data

A teacher asked 8 students how many hours they sleep each night. Here are the results:

7, 8, 6, 9, 7, 8, 7, 6

Calculate the following in your notebook:

  1. The total hours of sleep.
  2. The average hours of sleep.
  3. The percentage of students who sleep 7 hours or more.
  4. Write one sentence that explains what your results mean.
💡
Hint: Total = 7 + 8 + 6 + 9 + 7 + 8 + 7 + 6. Count = 8 students. Then Average = Total ÷ 8. Students who sleep ≥ 7 hours: count them, then divide by 8 and multiply by 100.

Key Takeaway

Processing data turns raw numbers into meaningful information. First, organize your data into a frequency table. Then, calculate the total, average, and percentage. Always remember to interpret your results — explain what the numbers actually mean. Next lesson, you'll use processed data to create alternatives and make decisions!
